Preface

Many people still think of C++ as the complicated language from the old C++98 days. That reputation was earned — and it is out of date. Over the last decade the language gained powerful features that make it easier to learn, safer to use, and much more enjoyable.

If you haven't used C++ recently, you might assume "modern C++" is a small update. It isn't. C++17, C++20, and C++23 introduced improvements that genuinely simplify development: smart pointers instead of naked new, algorithms and ranges instead of hand-rolled loops, std::expected instead of error-code spaghetti, and compile-time checks — concepts, constexpr — that catch mistakes before your program ever runs. The language still gives you full control of the machine; it now also helps you keep that control safely.

I believe a good practical learning book can help self-learners jump into modern C++ quickly. That belief is the reason this book exists — and the reason it is free.

This book is written for the developer who needs to use C++ professionally: to build SDKs and libraries other people depend on, to write code that is safe by construction and testable by design, and to ship it on Linux, macOS, and Windows. It is deliberately not a language-lawyer's tour. Every day teaches the idioms working C++ engineers actually reach for, and every day ends with exercises, a quiz, and a small project so the material sticks.

Each chapter is one “day.” Twenty-one honest days of work — roughly two to four focused hours each — will take you from an empty machine to a small, tested, documented, cross-platform SDK with continuous integration. You can also spread the days over weeks; the structure works either way.

The book is written in tribute to a technical book that taught me C more than twenty years ago and shaped the way I learn programming — daily chapters, worked examples, exercises, and a small project you actually build. It is published incrementally and in the open, and like every open-source project, it becomes good because of the people who check it. If you find an error, an unclear passage, or a better example, please say so; see About the Author for where.
